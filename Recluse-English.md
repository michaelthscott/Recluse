# RECLUSE

There are always decisions which need to be made. They can be fine or broad in compass, but ultimately things come down to two major problems: what to paint and how to paint it. With regard to the latter, Michael Scott has set up a system for these paintings which, to all intents and purposes, makes that decision for him. The interesting thing, though, is that in so concentrating on the how, he has found a way of allowing subject matter to establish itself. By laying down procedures which require to be rigorously followed and by then adhering to their stipulations, what these paintings can be thought to represent is seen to grow out of the activity of making them.

The genesis of the RECLUSE series lay in Scott's researches into two areas; firstly, the music of John Cage, and secondly, the theory and criticism of Chinese painting. What one encounters in both is an activity which is governed by a simple set of rules. In Cage's work chance is able to distinguish itself from chaos by virtue of its operating within clearly defined parameters; and classic Chinese painting is, at root, guided by the Six Laws written down by Hsieh-Ho in the early sixth century. For the RECLUSE paintings, specific facts within these fields are of less importance than the general principle drawn from them. That is, that the creative process takes place within a system which is highly structured. The preordination of a set of procedures allows chance or individuality to operate in the making of each work. One knows the kind of thing that will be produced, but its particular configurations, the predominant colours, its ultimate surface qualities, are infinitely variable.

There is an extensive literature on Chinese painting. The Six Laws have been constantly readdressed and there has built up a well-defined sense of the proprieties of the activity. This extended meditation upon a small number of what are for the most part commonsense rules about composition, application of colour, use of appropriate historical and stylistic models, and so on, is due to an awareness that painting's function extends far beyond its ability to picture the visible world. Subject categories have been exhaustively examined in relation to their ability to embody the principles of one or other of the major Chinese thought systems. Confucianism, Taoism and Buddhism are accommodated within painting - and accommodate painting within themselves - in subtly differing ways. The title of this series of paintings, RECLUSE, refers to this, to the efforts of Chinese painting to embody the unwordliness of its religious and ethical thought. The parallel might be to a contemporary dissatisfaction with superficial pleasures of urban living and a preference for the more profound spiritual appeal of nature and the countryside. To designate them landscape paintings would not be a misnomer, but they are produced in a thoroughly abstract manner. They are, in a sense, a Chinese painting in reverse: rather than attaining the spiritual through close attention to the real, they encounter the real by means of the abstract world of mathematics.

One should perhaps explain the working method. When returning from a recent visit to Japan, Scott picked up at the airport a pocket calculator. Using this he devised a sequence of programmes which, when fed with figures generated by the calculator's random number function, would fix the parameters of a painting. (I have called them paintings and for want of a more precise term will continue to do so, but strictly speaking they are wall-mounted constructions. The painted board which is their surface covers a substantial and carefully made wooden carcass.) Their overall area, derived ultimately from Scott's arm span, remains constant, but beyond that all aspects of the configuration are decided by an electronic throw of the dice'. Their height to width ratio, the depth, the dimensions of the elaborately crafted support structure, the details of the surface to be painted, each of these elements is decided upon by the use of a programme.

They are not true rectangles. Three sides deviate from the orthogonal by a few millimetres, but there is always one edge designated to stay vertical or horizontal. This tourth, right line anchors the work, letting the nuanced shifts from the rectilinear have their full effect. The surface is articulated by a skewed grid of co-ordinates, each of which is marked either by an absence - a hole drilled through the board, a presence - a dowel peg inserted into the board, or something in between - a half-hole. In addition there are up to three rectangular studs of the same proportions as the panel but a fraction of its size. The value of this fraction, and the positions in which these insignia', as Scott calls them, are placed, are again determined by the pocket computer.

Once this structure has been determined it is ready for paint to be applied and for Scott to 'attack' it with a variety of instruments, blunt and otherwise. The actions by which he works into the paint are entirely unpremeditated, but the particular colours of pigment used, and the order in which they are applied is, once more, not something which Scott has to decide. It is given information which leads, finally, to the rich surfaces we see, surfaces that offer up meanings which extend far beyond a dry description of the mechanics of their construction. The system is an abstraction, but that the RECLUSE paintings are landscapes seems, from the first, to be beyond doubt. To be sure, they are a typology of landscape rather than a set of views, but they nonetheless operate in the realm of physical, as well as mental experience. The kinds of scenes one imagines they might be - and it is a case of them 'invoking' as much as 'seeming to represent' - are richly varied. In spite of their consistent surface area, one's sense of the scale in which they relate to their putative subject differs widely from work to work. The fractured yellows and greens of the tall, thin RECLUSE (1) are close to, dense and tangled; the earthier, all-over green of RECLUSE (3) is seen as from afar, the sloping upper edge of this wide horizontal panel acting as a skyline; the reds and blues of RECLUSE (2) suggest more fluid elements and, in concert with that, a shifting focal depth.

Part of the fascination which painting is understood to have in China comes from the knowledge that one has the power to represent vast tracts of the natural world on relatively infinitesimal areas of silk. Whole mountain ranges can be encompassed in the space of a few square inches. The 'insignias' which emboss each RECLUSE suggest this aspect and redouble its significance. Insofar as the paintings capture an aspect of nature for us, the Insignias are, within the embodiment itself, emblematic of the means through which that capture is effected.

Of Hsieh-Ho's Six Laws of Painting, the first alone, 'animation through spirit onsonance', has been understood to be unteachable:

> *'Spirit consonance necessarily involves an innate knowledge; it assuredly cannot be secured through cleverness or close application, nor will time aid its attainment. It is an unspoken accord, a spiritual communion, something that happens without one's knowning how'.'* *

Scott was born and spent his childhood in the countryside of Northern Ireland. There is a sense, always, that what one is - one's history will find expression in and through what one does. Using the universality of the mathematical function and the anonymity of randomly generated numbers allows the potential insularity of this experience, its non-communicability to others, to appear in a more amenable form. The vitality of these works lies in their being able, through the thoroughly detached nature of the 'how' of their making, to give form to personal experience rather than to picture it, and in so doing to offer a more profound space for one's reflections upon the world.

**Michael Archer**

\* Kuo Jo-hsu, early 11th century, translated in Susan Bush and Hsio-yen Shih, Early Chinese Texts on Painting, Harvard, 1985
